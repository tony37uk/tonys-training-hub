📖 How-To Guide

Writing Better Prompts

⏱️ 15–20 minutes 🟢 Beginner friendly 🤖 Works with any AI tool 🗓️ Updated November 2026

The quality of the response you get from an AI tool like Claude, ChatGPT or Copilot depends heavily on the quality of your prompt. A vague question gets a vague answer. A clear, well-briefed prompt gets something genuinely useful. This guide walks you through the key principles with real before-and-after examples.

🎯

What you'll learn: How to structure a prompt, give the right context (including why you want something), use examples well, lay out longer prompts, get better answers to hard problems, and avoid the most common mistakes.

🔬 Anatomy of a great prompt

Most great prompts share the same building blocks. You don't need all of them every time, but knowing each one helps you decide what to include.

Role You are an experienced IT trainer writing for a non-technical audience.
Context I'm creating a beginner guide for colleagues who have never used GitHub before.
Task Write a step-by-step guide for setting up GitHub Pages.
Why It'll go on our intranet, and people will follow it on their own without anyone to ask.
Format Use numbered steps, keep each step to 2–3 sentences, and finish with a short troubleshooting tip.
Example Here's the tone I want: "Click the green button in the top right — it's the one labelled New repository."
💡

You don't need all six parts every time. For a quick question, just the task might be enough. For complex or important work, add as many parts as you can. A quick test: if you handed your prompt to a colleague who knows nothing about the task, would they know exactly what to do? If they'd be confused, the AI will be too.

🎯 Principle 1 — Be specific, not vague

The single biggest improvement most people can make is simply being more specific. AI tools are very good at following precise instructions, but they'll make assumptions when you leave things unclear.

1

Say exactly what you want

Include the topic, the purpose, the audience and any constraints. The more specific you are, the less guessing the AI has to do. If you want something ambitious, say so: AI tools often do the minimum unless you ask for more.

❌ Vague

"Write something about Azure."

✅ Specific

"Write a 200-word plain-English summary of what Azure is, aimed at someone who has never used cloud computing before."

2

Specify the length and format

If you don't say how long or what format you want, the AI will guess. Tell it upfront.

❌ No format

"Explain how Intune works."

✅ With format

"Explain how Intune works in 5 bullet points, each one a single sentence, suitable for a team briefing."

3

Say what to do, not just what to avoid

"Don't" instructions tell the AI what you don't want but leave it guessing about what you do want. Describe the result you're after instead.

❌ Only a "don't"

"Don't use bullet points or jargon."

✅ Says what to do

"Write it as two short paragraphs of everyday language, the way you'd explain it to a friend over coffee."

🗂️ Principle 2 — Give context

Context helps the AI tailor its response to your actual situation. Think of it like briefing a new colleague: the more background you give them, the better their work will be.

4

Explain who the output is for

The same question gets very different answers depending on the audience. Always mention who will be reading or using the output.

❌ No audience

"Explain what a DNS record is."

✅ With audience

"Explain what a DNS record is to someone who works in HR and has no IT background. Use a simple everyday analogy."

5

Share relevant background information

If you're working on something specific — a project, a document, a system — tell the AI about it. Most tools now let you attach files or screenshots directly, which is quicker and more reliable than describing them.

❌ No background

"Write an email about the system outage."

✅ With background

"Write a brief email to end users explaining that our M365 email service was down from 9am–11am today due to a Microsoft outage, and that all services have now been restored. Keep it under 100 words and professional in tone."

6

Explain why, not just what

When you give the reason behind an instruction, the AI can apply it sensibly to situations you didn't spell out. A bare rule only covers the exact case you wrote down.

❌ Rule only

"Keep sentences short."

✅ Rule plus reason

"Keep sentences short — many of our readers use English as a second language and read these on their phones."

🎭 Principle 3 — Assign a role

Telling the AI what kind of expert to be helps set the tone, vocabulary and level of depth. A single sentence is enough. A role works best alongside good context, not instead of it: "for HR staff with no IT background" usually matters more than an impressive job title.

7

Use "You are a..." to set the perspective

Start your prompt by assigning a role. This works especially well for writing tasks, technical explanations, reviews and training content.

❌ No role

"Create a quiz about Azure fundamentals."

✅ With role

"You are an experienced Microsoft certified trainer. Create a 5-question multiple choice quiz testing basic Azure fundamentals for people starting the AZ-900 course. Include the correct answers at the end."

📋 Principle 4 — Use examples

Showing the AI an example of what you want — even a rough one — is one of the most powerful techniques available. It removes ambiguity instantly.

8

Show, don't just tell

If you have an existing document, email or style you want the AI to match, paste it in and say "write something in this style." This works better than trying to describe the style in words.

❌ No example

"Write a brief, friendly, informal guide step."

✅ With example

"Write a guide step in this style: 'Head to Settings — it's the gear icon on the left sidebar. Don't worry if yours looks slightly different; the layout can vary depending on your plan.'"

For repeated tasks, give 3–5 examples that differ from each other. With only one, the AI may copy it too closely — the same length, the same opening words, even the same topic.

🧱 Principle 5 — Structure longer prompts

Once a prompt includes pasted text, documents or several instructions, the AI needs to tell them apart. Clear structure stops it mistaking your source material for your instructions.

9

Label the parts of your prompt

Use headings, or simple tags like <document> and <instructions>, to separate each part. The tag names don't matter much; just keep them descriptive and consistent.

<document>
[paste the policy here]
</document>

<instructions>
Summarise this policy for new starters in 5 bullet points.
Flag anything that needs action in their first week.
</instructions>
10

Long documents first, question last

When you paste or attach something long, put it at the top and your question or instructions at the end. For long reports, it also helps to ask the AI to pick out the most relevant quotes first and then answer based on them.

🧠 Principle 6 — Let it think on hard problems

For analysis, troubleshooting, planning or anything with several steps, the AI gives better answers when it works through the problem before committing to a conclusion.

11

Ask it to reason, then check its work

Many tools now have a "thinking", "extended thinking" or "reasoning" mode — switch it on for tricky problems. If yours doesn't, ask it to think things through before answering. Asking it to check the answer against your requirements at the end catches a lot of mistakes.

❌ Straight to an answer

"Which licence should we buy?"

✅ Room to think

"We're a 40-person charity using M365 Business Basic and need Intune for laptops. Think through the licence options and their trade-offs, then recommend one. Before you finish, check your recommendation covers every requirement I listed."

🔁 Principle 7 — Iterate and refine

You don't have to get the perfect prompt first time. The best results often come from a short back-and-forth where you build on the AI's first response.

12

Use follow-up prompts to refine

If the first response isn't quite right, don't start over. Tell the AI what to change. Be specific about what you liked and what needs adjusting.

  • → "That's good but make it shorter — aim for half the length."
  • → "Rewrite the second paragraph in a less formal tone."
  • → "Add a practical example to point number 3."
  • → "Can you turn this into a table instead?"
  • → "Great — now adapt this for a technical audience."
13

Let the AI help you write the prompt

If you're not sure what to include, ask the AI to interview you first, or to improve a prompt you've drafted. It's often good at spotting what's missing.

  • → "Before you start, ask me any questions you need answered to do this well."
  • → "Here's a prompt I've written. How would you improve it so the result is more useful?"
💡

Think of prompting like briefing a very capable assistant. You wouldn't expect a colleague to get a complex piece of work perfect from a one-line instruction — and neither should you expect that from AI.

⚠️ Common mistakes to avoid

!

Being so brief that the detail goes missing

Short prompts feel efficient, but they leave the AI guessing. Politeness is fine either way — what matters is that the task, audience and constraints are all there.

!

Cramming unrelated tasks into one prompt

Today's AI tools handle multi-part requests well when you number them clearly. But if the tasks are unrelated, or each one is big, split them up or work through them one step at a time so you can check each result before moving on.

!

Accepting the first response without reviewing it

AI can produce confident-sounding answers that contain errors or miss the point. Read the response critically, fact-check anything important, and push back. It helps to ask it to say when it isn't sure, and to point to where its information comes from.

!

Starting from scratch every time

If you keep typing the same background ("I work in IT support for a 200-person firm…"), save it. Most tools have custom instructions, Projects or saved prompts, so your context is there every time without retyping it.

⚠️

Important: AI tools can get facts wrong or make things up (known as "hallucinating"). Always verify important information from a trusted source before using it in official documents or communications. The AI Basics videos on why LLMs hallucinate and on RAG explain why this happens.

⚡ Quick reference — prompt starters

Copy and adapt these prompt templates to get started quickly:

TaskPrompt starter template
Explain a concept Explain [topic] in plain English for someone who [audience description]. Keep it under [length].
Write a guide You are an IT trainer. Write a step-by-step guide for [task]. Audience: [description]. It will be used [where/why]. Format: numbered steps.
Summarise a document [attach or paste the document first] Summarise the key points in [X] bullet points for [audience]. Focus on [specific aspect].
Write an email Write a [formal/informal] email to [audience] about [topic]. Keep it under [length]. Key points to include: [list]
Create a quiz Create a [X]-question multiple choice quiz on [topic] for [audience]. Include answers and a one-line explanation for each at the end.
Review/improve writing Review the following text for clarity and tone. Suggest improvements but keep the overall structure: [paste text]
Solve a tricky problem [describe the situation and what you've tried] Think through the likely causes, then suggest fixes in order of likelihood.
Get help with the prompt I want to [goal]. Ask me any questions you need answered first, then write the best prompt for it.
